# Interview Summary & Insights

## Summary

The participant's experience with team formation has changed based on her previous group-work experience. Initially, she prioritised forming teams with people she felt comfortable with. However, after experiencing a situation where she had to carry most of the workload because she was the only member with relevant Geographic Information Science knowledge, she became more intentional about selecting teammates.

She now prioritises people who are **ambitious, active, approachable, and capable of contributing**. She also considers whether potential teammates can complement her own skills and help the team achieve a good result.

The participant prefers teams where **everyone actively participates** in discussions and assignments. She sees disengagement, poor attendance, and unequal contribution as major problems in group work.

Although she prefers choosing teammates herself, she acknowledges that randomised team allocation can work when team members are approachable and active. If random allocation is necessary, she believes **peer evaluation** can help ensure that individual contributions are recognised fairly.

---

## Key Insights

### 1. Previous team experiences influence future team formation

The participant changed how she selects teammates after experiencing an unbalanced team where she had to carry much of the workload.

> "I feel like I'm carrying the whole thing."

**Insight:**  
Students may develop specific criteria for selecting teammates based on negative or positive experiences from previous group projects.

**Design implication:**  
A team-matching system could allow students to identify what they value in teammates, such as reliability, activity level, skills, or working style.

---

### 2. Students value active and reliable teammates

The participant repeatedly emphasised that her ideal teammates should be **active, ambitious, approachable, and willing to participate**.

> "I think the ideal team for a class project is a team where all of us are active."

**Insight:**  
Team formation is not only about finding people with the right skills. Students also need confidence that potential teammates will actually contribute.

**Design implication:**  
The system could provide information about potential teammates' collaboration preferences or behaviours, such as availability, preferred roles, communication style, and previous peer feedback.

---

### 3. Skill complementarity is important when forming teams

The participant previous experience showed her the difficulty of being the only person with a particular area of expertise.

She therefore wants teammates who can also teach her rather than relying on her to teach everyone else.

> "It's not just me teaching people; they can also teach me."

**Insight:**  
Students may benefit from teams where members have **complementary skills**, rather than simply grouping people with similar interests or backgrounds.

**Design implication:**  
A team-matching system could identify missing skills within an existing team and recommend students who possess those skills.

**Example:**  
If a team already has members with programming, research, and business skills but lacks a designer, the system could recommend students with relevant design skills.

---

### 4. Students want autonomy when choosing teammates

When asked how she would form teams as a tutor, the participant said:

> "I would just let people choose by themselves."

**Insight:**  
Students may prefer having control over who they work with rather than being automatically assigned to a team.

**Design implication:**  
The system should function as a **recommendation tool rather than an automatic assignment system**, allowing students to discover potential teammates and make the final decision themselves.

---

### 5. Random team allocation is not necessarily the problem

The participant was randomly assigned to her current team but had a positive experience because the members were approachable and active.

> "Fortunately, the people in the team, they are really approachable. They are really active."

**Insight:**  
Random allocation itself may not be problematic. The bigger issue is the uncertainty about whether randomly assigned teammates will be compatible and willing to contribute.

**Design implication:**  
Instead of completely replacing random allocation, a system could help reduce uncertainty by showing relevant information about potential teammates before or during team formation.

---

### 6. Peer evaluation can help address unequal contribution

The participant described using peer evaluation during her bachelor's degree to deal with a teammate who contributed poorly.

> "When it came to the group review, she received a bad score."

**Insight:**  
Peer evaluation can provide a mechanism for recognising unequal contribution and making assessment feel fairer.

**Design implication:**  
If teams are randomly assigned, the system could support or integrate peer feedback mechanisms to help instructors identify contribution issues.
