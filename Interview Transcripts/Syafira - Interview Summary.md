# Interview Summary & Key Insights

## Interview Context

**Topic:** How students form teams for class projects  
**Participant:** Student  
**Focus:** Team formation behaviour, selection criteria, team dynamics, negative experiences, and possible team-formation methods.

---

## 1. Interview Summary

The participant's experience forming teams depends heavily on whether they already know someone in the class. When they do not know anyone, they usually look for teams that are not yet complete and approach them directly to ask whether they need another member.

The participant generally approaches people they have seen in previous classes, even if they do not know them personally or know their names. When selecting a team, they also pay attention to whether a team appears to still have an available spot. They do not usually approach multiple teams simultaneously or reconsider their first choice, although they described one situation where a team asked them to wait before deciding. By the following week, that team had already become complete, forcing the participant to approach another team.

The participant described both positive and negative team experiences. A positive team is characterised by active engagement, open communication, mutual understanding, and the ability to discuss both project-related and personal topics. A negative team is characterised mainly by poor communication, where people may talk without listening to each other. The participant also tends to avoid classmates who talk excessively without listening to others.

When the participant had no choice but to approach another team, they were fortunate that the second team was open to including them. However, the team still needed to confirm with the tutor whether adding another member was permitted.

If the participant were a tutor responsible for creating teams, they would try to create groups with diverse backgrounds, such as different courses or majors. They believe this could give team members more perspectives to discuss.

The participant was also familiar with randomised team formation, where students are assigned numbers and grouped based on those numbers. They saw this as a possible method for forming teams.

Overall, the participant described team formation as involving mixed feelings. It can be exciting to meet and work with new people, but it can also be intimidating when students do not know each other.

---

# 2. Key Insights

## Insight 1 — Team formation starts with social familiarity

The participant's first instinct is to approach people they have previously seen in class. Familiarity does not necessarily mean they are friends; simply recognising someone from previous classes is enough to make approaching them feel more comfortable.

**Evidence:**
- The participant approaches people they have seen in previous classes.
- They may not know the person's name or anything about them personally.

**Analysis:**

This suggests that students may rely on weak social ties when forming teams. Even limited familiarity can reduce the uncertainty of approaching a stranger.

---

## Insight 2 — Students use visible team status to decide whom to approach

The participant actively looks for teams that appear to be incomplete before approaching them.

**Evidence:**
- They look for a team that is "not yet completed."
- They observe whether a team appears to have an available position.

**Analysis:**

Team formation is partly a visual and situational decision. Students need to determine who is still available before initiating an interaction. This can become difficult when team status is unclear or changes quickly.

---

## Insight 3 — Team formation can create uncertainty and require "gambling"

When students do not know their classmates well, the participant feels they have to take a chance when choosing someone.

**Evidence:**
- The participant says they sometimes have to "gamble on it."
- They may choose someone based on the assumption that the person might work well in a team.

**Analysis:**

Students often have limited information about potential teammates. They may want to know whether someone is communicative, reliable, engaged, or compatible, but these qualities are difficult to assess before working together.

This creates a gap between **who is socially approachable** and **who is actually a good teammate**.

---

## Insight 4 — Delayed responses can cause students to lose team opportunities

One important negative experience occurred when the participant approached a team during the first week and was told that the team would think about it later. By the following week, the team had already become complete.

**Evidence:**
- The participant was asked to wait.
- When they returned the following week, the team was already full.
- They then had to approach another team.

**Analysis:**

Team formation is time-sensitive. A student may believe that an opportunity remains available when, in reality, the team can become unavailable quickly.

This creates a potential problem around **unclear commitment and changing team availability**.

---

## Insight 5 — Good team dynamics are primarily about communication

The participant's definition of a good or bad team strongly centres on communication.

### Bad team
- People do not listen to each other.
- Someone may want to talk while another person is unwilling to listen.
- Communication becomes difficult.

### Good team
- Everyone has an opportunity to speak.
- Members understand each other.
- Members show empathy towards one another.

**Analysis:**

For this participant, team quality is not primarily determined by academic ability. Interpersonal communication and mutual understanding are central to whether a team is perceived as successful.

---

## Insight 6 — The participant evaluates potential teammates based on observable behaviour

Although the participant does not have much information about classmates, they still form judgements based on what they observe in class.

For example, they may avoid people who talk excessively and do not appear to listen to others.

**Analysis:**

Students already perform informal teammate evaluation before forming groups. However, this evaluation is based on limited and potentially incomplete social cues.

This suggests an opportunity to support students in making more informed team decisions without forcing them to rely entirely on assumptions.

---

## Insight 7 — Different teams can have very different interaction dynamics

The participant is currently involved in two teams and describes noticeably different experiences.

One team is:
- Highly engaged
- Comfortable discussing the project
- Able to discuss personal topics
- More socially connected

The other team is:
- Quieter
- More difficult when making decisions
- Dominated by someone who talks a lot
- More reliant on voting to choose between ideas

**Analysis:**

Team formation does not end once students are assigned to a group. The resulting group dynamics can significantly influence collaboration, communication, and decision-making.

---

## Insight 8 — Random assignment can remove some of the burden of choosing

The participant mentioned a randomised method used in their home country, where students count off numbers and are grouped according to their numbers.

**Analysis:**

Random assignment can reduce the social pressure associated with choosing teammates. Instead of students having to approach others and potentially face rejection, the responsibility for group formation is shifted to the system or tutor.

However, randomisation also removes students' ability to consider compatibility, communication style, or complementary skills.

---

## Insight 9 — The participant values diversity in team composition

If responsible for forming teams, the participant would intentionally create groups with members from different backgrounds, such as different courses or majors.

**Analysis:**

The participant sees diversity as a potential source of richer discussion and perspectives. This suggests that team formation could consider not only availability but also complementary backgrounds and skills.

---

## Insight 10 — Team formation involves mixed emotional experiences

The participant describes forming teams with unfamiliar classmates as both exciting and scary.

**Analysis:**

There is an emotional dimension to team formation that goes beyond simply finding available members. Students may experience:

- Excitement about meeting new people
- Anxiety about approaching unfamiliar classmates
- Uncertainty about whether they will be accepted
- Uncertainty about whether potential teammates will work well together

This emotional uncertainty may be particularly relevant when students have limited existing social connections in a class.

---

# 3. Emerging Problem Space

The interview suggests that team formation is not simply a matter of **finding people who are available**.

Students may need to simultaneously:

1. **Identify available teams**
2. **Decide whom they feel comfortable approaching**
3. **Assess potential teammates with limited information**
4. **Deal with uncertainty about acceptance**
5. **Coordinate around changing team availability**
6. **Understand whether team composition is compatible**
7. **Navigate tutor rules around group size**
8. **Manage the social and emotional pressure of approaching others**

A potential design opportunity therefore exists around **reducing uncertainty during team formation while still allowing students to retain agency over who they work with**.

---

# 4. Potential Design Opportunities

Based on this interview alone, several areas could be investigated further:

### Team availability visibility
Make it easier for students to understand which teams are still open and how many members they need.

### Team compatibility information
Provide meaningful information about potential teammates, such as interests, working preferences, skills, or communication styles.

### Structured team matching
Support students in finding teammates based on complementary backgrounds, skills, or preferences rather than relying only on familiarity.

### Low-pressure team joining
Reduce the awkwardness and rejection associated with directly approaching an existing team.

### Tutor-supported formation
Allow tutors to establish constraints such as team size while still giving students some choice.

### Transparent team status
Prevent situations where students think a team is still available but it becomes full before they receive a response.

---

# 5. Key Takeaway

The strongest theme from this interview is **uncertainty**.

The participant often has limited information about classmates, teams can change quickly, and students must make decisions based on incomplete social cues. Team formation therefore involves both a **practical problem (finding an available team)** and a **social problem (deciding who might be a good teammate and feeling comfortable approaching them)**.

The interview suggests that a useful team-formation experience should not simply automate group assignment. Instead, it could help students **see their options, understand potential teammates, and make more informed decisions while reducing the social uncertainty involved in approaching others**.
